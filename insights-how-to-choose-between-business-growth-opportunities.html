How Do I Choose Between Several Good Opportunities for My Business?

When Every Option Has Potential, Look at What Each One Will Create.

Your business is successful, and several opportunities are in front of you. They're all legitimate. You have the resources to pursue any one of them. But you can't pursue all of them well.

One could generate considerably more revenue. Another could increase the long-term value of the company. A third could make the business less dependent on you.

So how do you decide which one deserves the yes?

Go back to the destination.

Before comparing returns, timelines or resources, I want to know where you're trying to go.

What do you want your life to look like? What do you want the business to make possible? What role do you want to play in it? Who do you want to spend your time with? Where do you want to be? What do you want an ordinary week to look like? What are you building toward over the next several years?

Once that destination is clear, we can look at each opportunity differently.

We're no longer asking only, “Which opportunity is best?”

What will each opportunity create?

Look at the life attached to each opportunity.

The opportunity that produces the most money could also require considerably more of your time.

That doesn't make it a bad opportunity. It means we need to understand what comes with it.

What will your role look like? What will it require from you? What happens to your time, responsibilities and other priorities if you pursue it?

The opportunity that increases the long-term value of the company raises different questions. What are you building that value for?

Do you eventually want to sell? Pass the company to your children? Build something you intend to lead for decades?

And the opportunity that makes the company less dependent on you isn't automatically the winner simply because it creates more freedom.

You may love being deeply involved in your company.

Freedom is valuable when it's the freedom you actually want.

Pay attention to the opportunity that makes you light up.

Sometimes the option that appears to fit the destination best isn't the one you're most excited about.

I don't want to dismiss that.

If you light up every time you talk about one particular opportunity, I want to know why.

Talk to me about why you light up. Let's explore that.

Your response gives us information.

It can reveal something we didn't capture when we established the destination. It can show us that what you want has changed. It can uncover an ambition, challenge or experience that matters more to you than you initially realized.

Then we look at the destination again. Is it still where you want to go? Do we need to establish a new destination? Can we create a blend of the two? Or can we build the opportunity differently so you're not forced to choose between two things that matter deeply to you?

Harder isn't automatically worse.

Sometimes the opportunity you want most is also the one that asks the most of you.

It requires more investment. More leadership. Better systems. A stronger team. Greater involvement from you for the next 18 months. That matters. But difficulty alone doesn't tell us that the opportunity is wrong.

Harder isn't automatically worse.

The challenge itself could be part of what excites you.

Before I make a recommendation, I want you to understand exactly what each choice requires and what it can create.

What will this require financially? What will it require operationally? What needs to change in the business? What will it require from you personally? What are the risks? What are the tradeoffs? And what needs to be built so the opportunity has the greatest chance of becoming what you want it to become?

Then we can make an informed decision rather than choosing the easiest path simply because it's easier.

The recommendation is mine. The decision is yours.

When I'm advising a business owner through a decision like this, I'm not simply presenting a list of options and leaving her to figure it out.

I'll analyze what I see. I'll challenge assumptions. I'll identify opportunities and risks. I'll explore alternatives. And I'll tell you what I recommend.

But I'm not going to decide what kind of success you should want. That's an important distinction.

More money isn't automatically better. More freedom isn't automatically better. A larger company isn't automatically better. And the easier path isn't automatically better.

Those things only become meaningful when we understand what you are trying to create.

The goal is to understand what each choice can create, what it will require, and how it affects the life and business you want to build.

Then the decision belongs to you.
