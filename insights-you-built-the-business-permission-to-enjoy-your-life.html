You Built the Business. Are You Giving Yourself Permission to Enjoy Your Life?

You've worked hard all year. You've answered the calls, handled the decisions, and carried the responsibility that comes with owning a business. Now the holidays are approaching, and the people you love are looking forward to spending time with you. You've made plans, and you've earned the opportunity to enjoy them, but there's still a part of your mind that won't leave the business alone.

Perhaps you're sitting at dinner when you remember an email you haven't answered, or you're watching your family enjoy a moment you helped make possible while wondering what will be waiting for you tomorrow. You may have the resources to take the trip, host the gathering, or buy the gifts, yet find it difficult to give the people around you the one thing they can't purchase: your undivided attention.

I know that burden. I've carried responsibilities that made stepping away feel impossible because I believed everything would fall apart without me. Even when I wanted to be present somewhere else, I felt the pull of everything that still needed my attention. The work didn't disappear simply because I closed my computer, and the responsibility followed me long after the working day ended.

Business ownership asks a great deal of us, and there are seasons when we willingly make sacrifices because we believe in what we're building. We take pride in being dependable. We want our clients to know they can count on us, and we want the business to succeed. But after years of making ourselves available, we can become so accustomed to carrying everything that stepping away begins to feel irresponsible.

That is the part I want business owners to examine. Not whether they've worked hard enough, or whether they deserve a vacation, but whether they've allowed themselves to enjoy the life their hard work was meant to support. When did being responsible for the business begin to mean that every other part of life had to wait?

The holidays make that question harder to ignore because they bring the people and experiences we care about into the same room as our unfinished work. We can tell ourselves that we'll relax after one more deadline or enjoy the next gathering when things settle down. But the people we love are living their lives now, and the moments we keep postponing don't wait for a more convenient business calendar.

Giving yourself permission to enjoy your life isn't a rejection of ambition. You can care deeply about your business and still want to be fully present for your family. You can value your clients and recognize that your own life deserves your attention, too. In fact, I believe those desires belong in the same conversation when we talk about what success should make possible.

Of course, permission alone doesn't remove every responsibility. If the business depends on you for every decision, you may have legitimate concerns about what happens when you're unavailable. That's where the conversation about capacity becomes important. A business can look successful from the outside while asking so much of its owner that the rewards of success remain just out of reach.

I'm not interested in telling established business owners to care less about what they've built. I want them to look closely at whether the business they operate today still supports the life they set out to create. As a consultant, I begin by understanding the business, the decisions that shaped it, and what the owner wants it to make possible from here. That conversation can reveal a very different kind of growth opportunity than simply finding more customers.

I've said before that the business was never the dream; it was the vehicle to finance the dream. The holidays offer a timely reminder of why that distinction matters. If you've spent the year building a business that provides for the people you love, I hope you'll also consider what it would mean to be fully present with them. And if enjoying that life feels harder than it should, that may be worth a conversation long after the decorations come down.

Elle Lundy
